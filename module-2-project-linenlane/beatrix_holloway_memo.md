1. Executive Summary

Variant B increased purchases from 1.85% to 2.10%, representing about 70 additional purchases per 28,000 emails, a 13.5% relative lift. This purchase-rate improvement was statistically significant (p = 0.034), while the 2.0% increase in click-through rate was not statistically significant (p = 0.47). Based on the purchase results, LinenLane should continue using Variant B as the leading option, while rerunning the test to build additional confidence and monitoring customer segments and return patterns for opportunities to improve marketing performance.

2. Findings

- Variant B produced more purchases.
  Variant B increased the purchase rate from 1.85% to 2.10%, or approximately 70 additional purchases per 28,000 emails. The relative lift was 13.5%, with a 95% confidence interval of +0.02 to +0.48 percentage points and a p-value of 0.034. In business terms, the results provide evidence that the difference in purchase rates is unlikely to be explained by random variation alone, although the size of the improvement remains uncertain.

- The click-rate improvement was small and uncertain.
  Variant B generated approximately 48 more clicks per 28,000 emails, increasing the click-through rate from 8.53% to 8.70%, a 2.01% relative lift. However, the 95% confidence interval ranged from -0.29 to +0.64 percentage points, and the p-value was 0.47. Because the interval includes zero, the analysis does not establish that Variant B consistently produces more clicks than Variant A.

- Customer segment analysis showed higher average order value among VIP customers.
  VIP customers had an average order value of $187, compared with $164 for Regular customers, a $23 difference per order. The 95% confidence intervals were $171–$203 for VIP customers and $156–$172 for Regular customers, with a p-value of 0.012. This suggests that customer value differs meaningfully by segment and supports examining whether VIP customers should receive more targeted marketing or retention efforts.

- Return analysis identified Damaged items as an area to investigate.
  Damaged returns had an average refund amount of $100.74, compared with $82.36 for Wrong Size returns, a difference of approximately $18.38. The 95% confidence intervals were $92.73–$108.74 for Damaged and $70.78–$93.94 for Wrong Size, with a Welch's t-test p-value of 0.010. This indicates that damaged returns are associated with higher average refund amounts and may warrant further investigation from both marketing and operations teams.

3. The A/B Test Result

Variant B should remain the leading option based on the purchase outcome. It produced approximately 70 more purchases per 28,000 emails, and the purchase-rate difference was statistically significant (p = 0.034). The click-rate result tells a different story: Variant B generated about 48 more clicks, but the result was not statistically significant (p = 0.47; 95% CI: -0.29 to +0.64 percentage points). Therefore, the purchase result provides stronger evidence for continuing with Variant B, while the click result should be treated as inconclusive rather than as proof of improved engagement. I recommend using Variant B for the next campaign while rerunning the test to confirm that the purchase improvement persists.

4. Recommendations

- Use Variant B for the next campaign because it produced the statistically significant improvement in purchase rate.
- Rerun the A/B test on the next campaign to determine whether the purchase-rate improvement continues and to increase confidence in the result.

- Monitor purchases rather than clicks as the primary success measure, since the purchase result showed clearer evidence of business impact.

- Explore targeted strategies for VIP customers, who had a higher average order value of $187 versus $164 for Regular customers.

- Investigate the causes and costs of Damaged returns, which had the highest average refund amount among the compared return categories.

6. Limitations and Next Steps

This analysis focused on the available campaign, customer, sales, and return data and does not establish the long-term impact of the marketing changes. A follow-up analysis should test Variant B across additional campaigns, examine performance by customer segment and marketing channel, and connect campaign results with revenue and customer lifetime value.
